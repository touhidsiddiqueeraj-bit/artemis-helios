Reviewer A:

1. The paper must provide a complete and reproducible power-stage model
The most important technical weakness is that the converter model is not described with sufficient detail to independently reproduce the reported MPPT results. The manuscript identifies a 50-Wp mono-Si PV module, a buck converter, IRFB4110 MOSFET, TC4420 gate driver, 100 μH/470 μF LC filter, 50-kHz switching frequency, and INA219 sensing, but these specifications alone are insufficient to establish a rigorous switching-converter simulation. The revised manuscript should provide the complete converter topology and all electrical parameters, including PV-side and battery-side operating ranges, MOSFET switching model, diode/body-diode characteristics, inductor DCR, capacitor ESR, switching/dead-time assumptions, gate resistance, driver supply voltage, PWM limits, current limits, sampling delays, sensor quantization, ADC/sensor filtering, controller execution time, and initial conditions. The authors should explicitly state whether the Simulink model is averaged, switching-level, or semiconductor-level and identify the exact solver, step size, simulation duration, and numerical tolerances. A complete schematic/block diagram with signal names and measurement points should be included. Without these details, the reported 94% MPPT efficiency cannot be independently reproduced, and the manuscript remains closer to an algorithmic simulation study than a rigorous power-electronics validation paper.

2. The claimed MPPT improvement requires stronger and fairer benchmark methodology
The reported 23.3-percentage-point improvement over plain P&O is impressive, but it requires more rigorous benchmarking before it can be interpreted as a controller-level advance. The manuscript reports 94.0% for the proposed method, 70.7% for P&O, 85.2% for VS-P&O, and 75.2% for incremental conductance under the monsoon scenario. However, the manuscript should demonstrate that all algorithms use identical PV models, sampling intervals, sensing resolution, converter limits, initialization, irradiance trajectories, battery constraints, and computational assumptions. Most importantly, the authors should include a controlled transient benchmark suite, not only stochastic full-day simulations. At minimum, evaluate step-down, step-up, ramp, cloud-edge, repeated cloud transients, and stochastic irradiance cases. Report settling time, maximum tracking error, overshoot/undershoot, energy-not-captured, oscillation amplitude around MPP, and tracking efficiency. A particularly useful experiment would compare the proposed controller against P&O and VS-P&O using exactly the same irradiance waveform and calculate instantaneous tracking error P
MPP−PPV. This would demonstrate whether the 23.3-point gain originates specifically from predictive pre-positioning rather than from differences in controller tuning or operating assumptions.

3. The control law and gain-scheduling mechanism require substantially deeper technical justification
The proposed control equation is interesting: the new reference is formed by blending the P&O reference and predicted MPP voltage, with adaptive weighting and a 15% deviation deadband. The manuscript gives the relationship Vref,new=(1−α)Vref,P&O+αVMPP,pred, followed by asymmetric cloud-clearing/cloud-drop weighting and a 20-step cooldown. However, the manuscript currently presents these parameters largely as selected design values rather than deriving them from control-theoretic or optimization principles. The authors should explain why 0.35 is selected as the nominal α, why the 0.20–0.55 region is considered robust, why 15% is appropriate for the triggering deadband, and why 20 control cycles are sufficient for cooldown. The α sensitivity analysis is useful, but it should be complemented by a multidimensional sensitivity analysis involving α, deadband, cooldown, prediction horizon, P&O step size, and UART delay. The paper should also discuss closed-loop stability or at least boundedness under prediction error. A predictive reference can destabilize or create oscillatory behaviour if the forecast is wrong or delayed; therefore, the authors should explicitly establish the conditions under which the predictive component remains subordinate to the stabilizing reactive controller.

4. The manuscript should quantify the complete control-loop latency rather than only MCU separation
The dual-MCU architecture is one of the paper's strongest ideas, but the claim that separating LSTM inference from PWM control prevents latency-induced jitter is not adequately demonstrated. The manuscript states that Helios runs LSTM inference while Artemis executes 100-ms MPPT updates and 50-kHz PWM, with UART communication between them. A reviewer needs actual timing measurements, not only architectural reasoning. Please report LSTM inference time distribution, preprocessing time, UART packet transmission time, packet parsing time, sensor acquisition time, control computation time, PWM update latency, worst-case execution time, and jitter. The authors should provide minimum/mean/maximum or percentile latency values over a long execution interval. Also clarify whether the 100-ms communication interval introduces an effective 100-ms prediction/control latency and how this interacts with cloud transients. If the LSTM predicts irradiance at a horizon shorter than or comparable to this latency, the benefit may be substantially different from what is claimed. A timing diagram showing irradiance → sensor → Helios → prediction → UART → Artemis → PWM → converter → PV response would greatly strengthen the paper.

5. Power semiconductor and thermal analysis is currently too superficial for the claimed hardware deployment
The manuscript reports a peak junction temperature of 62 °C and approximately 0.27 W conduction loss based on an assumed RDS(on), but this is insufficient to establish semiconductor reliability. The power-stage analysis should include both conduction and switching losses, especially because the MOSFET operates at 50 kHz and the gate driver is explicitly part of the architecture. The authors should calculate P
cond, Psw, gate-drive loss, diode/body-diode conduction loss, inductor copper/core losses, capacitor losses, and total converter loss. The temperature calculation should use a clearly defined thermal network, including junction-to-case, case-to-heatsink, heatsink-to-ambient, PCB thermal paths, and actual airflow/environmental assumptions. The stated effective Rth,JA should be justified rather than assumed. A loss breakdown and efficiency-versus-load curve should be added. This is particularly important because the paper presents a low-cost controller for rural SHS deployment; reliability under elevated ambient temperature, enclosure conditions, dust, and prolonged operation is more relevant than a single nominal simulated junction temperature.

6. The paper should distinguish MPPT efficiency from converter/system efficiency much more rigorously
The manuscript correctly notes that the 79% field performance ratio from a 122.4-kW Dhaka PV installation is not directly comparable to its 91.3% simulated MPPT tracking efficiency. Nevertheless, the paper should go further and establish a precise definition of the proposed metric. The equation for MPPT tracking efficiency must be explicitly stated, including the reference P MPP, integration interval, treatment of low-irradiance periods, converter losses, battery charging constraints, and any clipping or saturation. If P
MPP is obtained from an analytical PV model while P PV comes from the converter simulation, the authors must make clear that this is a controller tracking metric, not an energy-conversion efficiency. A separate metric should be reported for converter efficiency, η conv=P out /P in , and ideally end-to-end PV-to-battery energy efficiency. This distinction is particularly important because the manuscript currently uses terms such as “yield benefit” and “annual benefit,” which could be interpreted as system energy yield rather than MPPT tracking improvement. The revised manuscript should avoid any claim that the controller produces a 23.3-percentage-point improvement in actual SHS energy yield unless that has been independently demonstrated.

Reviewer B:

The manuscript has several genuinely interesting engineering elements:
- dual-MCU separation of AI prediction and deterministic control;
- LSTM-assisted predictive MPPT;
- variable-step P&O fallback;
- explicit cloud-transient-aware blending;
- low-cost embedded implementation;
- stochastic irradiance generation;
- field-informed model validation;
- Monte Carlo evaluation;
- explicit sensitivity analysis.

The architecture is described as ESP32-S3 for prediction/logging and STM32F103 for real-time control, with 100-ms UART communication and 50-kHz PWM switching. That partitioning is potentially a worthwhile contribution to renewable-energy power electronics and embedded control systems. However, the paper's central evidentiary limitation is decisive: The proposed MPPT controller has not yet been experimentally validated as a closed-loop hardware system.

The field campaign validates an irradiance logger/model, not the actual MPPT power converter. The manuscript itself acknowledges this limitation.

Therefore, the authors should not attempt to hide this limitation. Instead, they should make the methodological distinction a strength:
- field-informed stochastic modelling + reproducible controller simulation + embedded architecture feasibility
- rather than claiming demonstrated field deployment performance.

The field irradiance instrumentation is not adequate to support broad claims without stronger qualification
The authors deserve credit for explicitly reporting that the BH1750 is sensor-grade rather than pyranometer-grade and that 2.6% of daytime readings were affected by saturation. Nevertheless, this limitation directly affects the paper's strongest empirical claim: validation of the synthetic irradiance model. The revised manuscript should provide the BH1750 measurement range, spectral response, cosine-response characteristics, calibration procedure, calibration uncertainty, temporal response, mounting geometry, and the rationale for converting lux-like sensor output into irradiance. The back-to-back glass calibration with ratio 0.9314 is useful, but it does not establish absolute radiometric accuracy. If possible, the authors should compare the BH1750 against a calibrated reference sensor during at least a subset of the campaign. At minimum, the manuscript should explicitly frame the field dataset as relative short-timescale irradiance variability data, rather than as high-accuracy absolute GHI measurements. This distinction matters because the LSTM input and stochastic-model calibration depend directly on measurement quality.

Reviewer C:

The Introduction has the requested logical components but should be rewritten into a tighter engineering argument
The current Introduction is commendably organized into Context, Problem Statement, State of the Art, Gap Analysis, Contribution, and Significance, which is already aligned with the requested logical flow. However, the sections are somewhat repetitive, and the contribution/significance material overlaps considerably. I recommend restructuring it into six compact paragraphs: (1) Context: explain the SHS power-electronics problem and why monsoon variability matters; (2) Problem: quantify the limitations of reactive MPPT under fast irradiance changes; (3) State of the Art: compare P&O/VS-P&O/INC, predictive MPPT, LSTM, lightweight AI, and multi-MCU architectures; (4) Gap: identify precisely what existing approaches do not simultaneously address—resource-constrained dual-plane control, cloud-independent prediction, field-informed stochastic validation, and low-cost deployment; (5) Contributions: give four numbered contributions without repeating numerical results excessively; (6) Significance: explain why the work matters to low-cost renewable-energy power electronics. The final paragraph should also explicitly state what the paper does not claim, namely full field validation of the controller. This would greatly improve clarity and scientific credibility.
